01

Story

What separates a story from a thing that happened

Story

A story is the record of what someone paid to get what they wanted.

Not
A sequence of events. An interesting situation. A thing that happened to you that felt significant at the time.
Test
Remove the character's inner life and leave the events. Does it still work? Then it's a report, not a story.

Three words get used interchangeably and shouldn't be. Keeping them apart is the difference between diagnosing a problem and guessing at it.

Narrative
Events in sequence. This, then this.
Plot
Events in causal order. This, therefore this.
Story
A plot that changes someone, and argues something about being alive.

E. M. Forster's demonstration is still the cleanest one available: the king died and then the queen died is narrative. The king died, and then the queen died of grief is plot. Two words of causation convert a list into a machine.

Minimum viable story Someone wants something. Something stands in the way. What they do about it changes who they are.

Plot is a selection, not a recording

Most of what happened is not in the plot. Plot is only the events that force a choice. This is the single most useful thing to remember when you're working from real life, where everything has equal weight until you decide otherwise.

Scene test
Test
Does someone make a decision here they can't take back? If no, it isn't a plot beat. It's texture — useful, but it can't carry weight.
02

Character

Revealed by choice, never by description

Character

Character is what a person does when the right choice costs them something.

Not
A list of traits, a backstory, a personality type, or a description of how someone comes across.
Test
Name a choice they made that cost them. Can't? Then you have a description, not a character.

There are two layers, and confusing them is why so many characters feel vivid on the page and hollow in the room.

Characterization
The surface. Job, voice, clothes, habits, the way they hold a cup. Everyone can observe it. It is not character — it's the packaging.
True character
Revealed only by choice under pressure. Invisible until something is at stake. The deeper the pressure, the deeper the revelation.

This is why "she's brave" is worthless and "she went back in" is everything. Pressure is the only instrument that reads true character, which is also why comfortable characters are boring — there's no signal.

Displacement

Displacement

The character is somewhere they don't belong.

Why
It generates friction continuously, with no event required. A character who is permanently a stranger is under low-grade pressure in every scene — which means every scene can reveal something.
Test
Where is this person a stranger? Physically, socially, in their role, in time, or inside their own life?
Physical
A new city. A room that isn't theirs.
Social
The table where everyone is more senior, richer, more fluent.
Role
Holding a position they don't believe they've earned.
Temporal
They've outgrown a place but haven't left it.
Internal
They don't belong in their own life anymore.
03

Motivation

Why the character can't simply stop

Motivation

Motivation is the reason walking away isn't available.

Not
A goal. A goal is what they're heading toward; motivation is what makes stopping impossible.
Test
Ask why they don't quit. If the honest answer is "they could, easily," the pursuit has no engine.

Motivation has four parts. Remove any one and the pursuit collapses into a hobby.

1 · Want
The external objective. Concrete, visible, winnable or losable. Drives the plot. "Get the job." "Finish the film." "Be taken seriously by that room."
2 · Misbelief
A past experience taught them a false rule about how to be safe, loved, or worth something. The want is usually a strategy the misbelief invented. Drives everything underneath.
3 · Need
What would actually make them whole. Usually unknown to them, and usually in direct conflict with the want. Drives the arc.
4 · Stakes
What is lost on failure. Specific and personal. Not "everything falls apart" — "he loses the one person who still believes he's honest."

And one modifier that decides whether any of it has pressure:

Urgency
Why today, and not any other day? A story that could have happened next month instead has no clock, and without a clock the character is just idling in their situation.

The chain

Wound→Misbelief→Want→Pursuit→Conflict→Cost→Recognition→Need met — or refused→Change

Read it backwards to build it: decide what change you want to prove, then work out what would have to be recognised, then what it would cost, then what could impose that cost, then what want would walk them into it, then what false rule generated that want.

The sharpest question in this whole document What would they have to admit for the want to disappear? That answer is the need — and the story is the process of making them admit it.
04

Conflict

Opposition, restraint, escalation

Conflict

Conflict is the collision of two forces that cannot both win.

Not
Obstacles. An obstacle delays; conflict opposes. A traffic jam is not conflict. A traffic jam is conflict only when someone chose to send you that way.
Test
Can you argue the other side convincingly? If the opposition is stupid, or evil for no reason, you have an obstacle wearing a face.

Name both forces explicitly. If you can only name one, you have a complaint.

Three levels

The strongest stories run all three at once, and the three mirror each other — the fight outside is the same shape as the fight inside.

Inner
Self against self. Two things they want that can't both be had. The most portable level, and the only one that's always available.
Personal
Against someone whose want is legitimate and incompatible. Note legitimate — villains who simply enjoy being wrong produce no tension.
External
Against a system, a place, a scarcity, or time itself.

Restraint

This is the part almost everyone leaves out, and it's the part that produces tension.

Restraint

Restraint is whatever prevents the easy solution.

Why
Conflict without restraint resolves in ten seconds — the character just fixes it. Tension is conflict held shut.
Test
"Why don't they just fix it?" The answer is the restraint. If the answer is "they could, they just haven't got round to it," there's no story yet.
Imposed
Money, distance, time, obligation, a physical limit, a rule they can't break. Reveals the world.
Chosen
A principle, a promise, pride, something they refuse to do. Reveals the character — which makes it the more interesting kind.
Tension formula Tension = restraint × time held. Break the restraint early and the middle doesn't sag — it's empty, because the pressure was already spent.

Escalation

Every attempt to solve the problem must make it worse, or cost more, or both. The opposition should get smarter, not merely bigger — bigger is volume, smarter is pressure.

Escalation test
Test
Try reordering your middle scenes. If nothing breaks, you have episodes, not escalation. In a real escalation, scene four is impossible before scene three.
05

Drama

And why it isn't the same as intensity

Drama

Drama is unresolved consequence: we care about someone, something matters to them, and the outcome is genuinely in doubt.

Not
Shouting, weeping, or intensity. Those are symptoms that may or may not indicate the condition.
Test
Remove all three ingredients one at a time. Whichever removal the scene survives, you didn't have.

The cleanest way to hold the distinction between drama and melodrama:

Drama
The stakes exceed the display. A man says "fine" and it costs him everything.
Melodrama
The display exceeds the stakes. A man weeps and we don't yet know why it matters, so we watch the weeping instead of feeling it.

Three kinds of not-knowing

These are not interchangeable, and most flat work is flat because it only ever reaches for the first one.

Surprise
The audience finds out when the character does. Cheap, effective for about two seconds, and unrepeatable.
Suspense
The audience knows more than the character. Expensive to set up, and it pays for minutes. Hitchcock's example: a bomb exploding under a table buys ten seconds of shock; letting the audience know it's there buys ten minutes of agony.
Tension
Stakes known, outcome uncertain, resolution deliberately withheld. This is the one restraint produces.
06

Emotion

Arranged, not performed

Emotion

Emotion is produced by arrangement, not by performance.

Why
You never transmit a feeling. You assemble the causes, show the visible effects, and the feeling occurs in the audience. This is the useful core of the Sanskrit rasa theory: the flavour arises in the viewer, never in the work.
Test
If you've told the audience what to feel, you've replaced the arrangement with an instruction — and instructions don't move anyone.

Two rules do most of the work here.

Inverse rule
What the character shows and what the audience feels are often opposite. A character holding it together devastates a room. A character sobbing does the feeling for us, and we feel less.
One register
Pick one dominant emotional register per piece and shade it with passing ones. Two dominants make a piece feel tonally broken, and the fix is always to demote one to a passing note.

The palette

Eight registers worth knowing by what they do to an audience, rather than by what they're called.

WonderOpens a curiosity gap. The strongest opening move there is.
CourageMakes people want to act. For effort, discipline, starting over.
SorrowThe strongest bonding register. Loss, failure, what can't be fixed.
HumourLowers defences, travels furthest. Weak on its own — needs something underneath it.
LongingBeauty, devotion, the thing just out of reach.
AngerHighest engagement, highest risk. Only works when a principle earns it.
FearEstablishes stakes. Aim it at "what I was afraid of," never at the audience.
PeaceA terrible opening and an unbeatable close.

One rule about the last one: peace has to be earned. If the restraint was imposed and still stands at the end, a calm resolution is a lie the audience will feel without being able to name. Sorrow or courage are the honest closes there.

07

Truth

The claim the ending proves

Truth

The truth is the claim about human life that the story demonstrates — and could have failed to demonstrate.

Not
A moral or a message. A moral is stated by someone; a truth is proved by what the ending costs and rewards.
Test
Write it as a sentence that could be false. If no reasonable person could disagree with it, it's a platitude, and platitudes are unfalsifiable by design.

Good: "People who chase respect become unable to accept it." Someone could argue with that. Bad: "Family is important." Nobody can.

The truth lives in the climax, not in the dialogue. Whatever the character chooses at the point of maximum pressure is what the story is arguing is true about people. If you want to change the theme, change the choice — not the speech.

Finding it from real material

An event isn't a story until it proves something. Say what happened, then ask so what? three times. The third answer is usually the actual subject.

Event
The rickshaw broke down; I was forty minutes late.
So what?
I rehearsed excuses the whole way there.
So what?
I've built an identity around being reliable.
So what?
I'm not disciplined — I'm frightened of looking careless. From outside, those two are identical.

That last line is the story. The rickshaw is just the thing that made it visible on a Tuesday.

08

Character arc

How a belief changes under pressure

Arc

An arc is the sequence by which a controlling belief is changed by pressure.

Not
Growth, or getting better at things, or announcing that you've learned something.
Test
An arc is proved by an action they could not have taken at the start. Saying "I've changed" is not an arc — it's a claim.

Six stages

1 · It works
The misbelief is operating, and it appears to be serving them.
2 · It's tested
First small cost. Easy to dismiss.
3 · They double down
Same strategy, more effort. This is the middle of your story.
4 · It fails
Everything built on the misbelief comes apart at once.
5 · The choice
Abandon the belief, or keep it and pay.
6 · The proof
An action impossible for them in stage one.

Three shapes

ShapeMovementFeels like
PositiveMisbelief → truth. They change.Satisfying. The default, and the one at most risk of being predictable.
NegativeMisbelief → deeper misbelief.Corruption or tragedy. Uncomfortable, and far more memorable.
FlatThey already hold the truth; the world changes around them.The natural shape for teaching, advocacy, and any story where the protagonist is the steady thing. Badly underused.

One more shape worth knowing because real life produces it constantly: the belief changes but the situation doesn't. They're in the same room, the same job, the same city — and they see it differently. It's quieter than the others and it's often the truest.

09

The seven parts

Beginning · break · commitment · middle · collapse · climax · end

Percentages are proportions, not rules. They scale: a ninety-second video and a two-hour film have the same shape, and the same failure points.

1Setup0–12%

Establish the normal that already contains the flaw. Show what they want, what's missing, and what they'll refuse to do about it. Plant the stakes and the restraint here so you never have to explain them later.

Job
Make the audience want something on the character's behalf — through their desire, their competence, or their vulnerability. Any one will do; all three is a lot.
Fails
Explaining the world before giving anyone a reason to care about it.
2The break~12%

The inciting event. Equilibrium ends. The measure is not size — it's irreversibility. A sentence someone says that can't be unheard is a break; a spectacular day that changes nothing is not.

Job
Pose the dramatic question the rest of the story answers. Will he get it? Will she admit it? Will they make it back?
Fails
Drama that leaves the situation exactly where it was.
3Commitment~25%

The character chooses to pursue. Usually after a refusal — the refusal is what proves the misbelief has real grip. Then the door shuts and normal stops being available.

Job
Convert a want into an active pursuit, by the character's own decision.
Fails
The character is carried along by events and never actually chooses. Passive protagonists are the most common structural fault there is.
4Escalation25–70%

The longest stretch, and where most stories die. Attempts fail in ways that make things worse. The opposition adapts. The restraint holds. Somewhere near the centre, a midpoint arrives that reframes the goal or flips the character from reacting to acting.

Job
Spend the character's options one at a time, so that by the end only the expensive one remains.
Fails
Repetition dressed as escalation — the same setback three times at the same volume.
5Collapse70–80%

The misbelief fails completely. Everything built on the old strategy comes apart, and the character is at maximum pressure — which is precisely why this is where true character becomes readable.

Job
Produce the crisis: a real dilemma. The best ones are between two goods or two evils, never between good and evil — that isn't a dilemma, it's an exam with an obvious answer.
Fails
A setback the character absorbs without it costing anything.
6Climax85–95%

The climax is a decision, not an explosion. The explosion is optional and always has been. This is the moment the character answers the dramatic question with an action, and in doing so tells us what the story believes.

Job
Four requirements, all mandatory: the protagonist acts rather than being rescued; the choice costs; it's a choice impossible for them in the setup; and it settles the question for good.
Fails
Someone else solves it. A coincidence solves it. Or nothing is given up, which means nothing was ever really at risk.
7The endlast 5%

Show the new normal — that's the proof the change is real. Show the price. Then stop.

Job
Let the audience arrive at the truth themselves, roughly one beat before you'd have said it.
Fails
Explaining the theme. Every sentence after the truth lands dilutes it.

11

Diagnostics

Symptom, cause, fix

When something feels wrong but you can't name it, the symptom is almost never the problem. Find the symptom below and read across.

SymptomActual causeFix
Nothing seems to happenNo irreversible break — events occur but the situation is restorable.Find what can't go back, or invent it.
The middle dragsThe restraint broke early, or attempts repeat instead of escalating.Hold the restraint longer. Make each attempt cost more than the last.
The character feels flatThey're described, not revealed — no choice under cost.Give them a decision where both options hurt.
The ending doesn't landThe protagonist didn't act, or nothing was given up.Make them choose, and charge them for it.
It feels preachyThe truth is stated rather than demonstrated.Delete the speech. Let the climax carry the argument.
It feels melodramaticEmotional display exceeds the stakes that earned it.Raise the stakes, or lower the volume. Usually the second.
Can't get investedNo stakes, or no vulnerability shown early.Name what's lost on failure — specifically, in the first 10%.
It feels randomBeats joined by "and then" rather than "but" or "therefore."List the beats. Put "but" or "therefore" between each pair. Cut whatever won't take one.
The opposition is weakThe other side has no legitimate case.Write their argument as if you agreed with it. Then use that version.
Emotionally hollowThe feeling was performed instead of arranged, or the audience was told what to feel.Cut the instruction. Show the cause and the visible effect; let them do the rest.

Build order is always the same — character, want, restraint, break, escalation, cost, choice, change, truth. Flavour comes last, and only after the chassis holds.
